Complexity arrives without being invited. A product begins with one clear thing it does well. Then someone asks for a second thing. Then a third. Then integration with something else. Each addition feels justified at the time. Each one has a reason. But at some point, the product stops being the thing it was and becomes a collection of reasons instead.

## The confusion at the root of it

There is a common assumption that complexity signals depth - that a thing with more features, more options, more settings is somehow more serious or more capable than a thing with fewer. This is wrong in a way that is worth examining carefully, because the wrongness is subtle.

Depth is about what a product can help you do. Complexity is about how much you have to navigate to do it. These are not the same thing. A product can be deeply capable and simple to use. A product can be enormously complicated and still only do one mediocre thing.

> Simplicity is not the absence of thought. It is the result of more of it.

## The discipline of removing

Adding features is easy. Every feature has an advocate - someone for whom it would be genuinely useful. The discipline is in asking whether its presence harms anyone else, and whether the product as a whole is better or worse for including it.

Most teams are good at deciding whether a feature should be built. Far fewer are good at deciding whether a feature should remain - whether it is still earning its place given what the product has become around it.

## Simplifying without losing meaning

Simplification is not reduction for its own sake. There is a version of simplicity that is just subtraction - removing things because they feel extraneous, without understanding what they were carrying. This produces products that are clean but thin.

Genuine simplification asks what each part is doing - not what it is, but what it is carrying - and then finds a way to carry the same weight with less structure. That is harder. It requires understanding what actually matters to the person using the thing.

The test for whether you have succeeded is not whether the product looks simple. It is whether the person using it feels clear.
